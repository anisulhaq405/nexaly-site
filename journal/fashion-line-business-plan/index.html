Write a fashion line business plan with a small collection, size-level stock, returns, launch costs and cash. Includes a filled financial example.

Example basis: All businesses, amounts and outcomes below are fictional planning examples in USD, prepared November 4, 2026. They are not industry benchmarks or customer results.

Define a collection you can actually produce

A fashion line business plan connects a customer hypothesis to design, samples, production quantities, fit, selling costs and cash. For an own-label collection, a generic retail plan is incomplete: patterns, samples, size distribution and minimum orders can commit money before the first customer buys.

This fictional launch offers two coordinated styles: an overshirt and trousers. Its hypothesis is that buyers want a small mix-and-match workwear range. That is an assumption to test with interviews, fitting feedback and paid orders, not evidence of demand. The plan names a launch owner, a supplier quote date and a decision date before expanding the range.

Opening cash of $6,000 leaves $1,900 after those commitments, before customer receipts and later selling costs. Landed cost here includes the assumed cost of bringing a garment into stock; validate freight, duties, packaging and rejected pieces against actual supplier terms instead of copying the $24 or $20 example.

Ask for minimum quantities by style, color and size, sample revision limits, delivery dates, payment milestones and a written quality-check process. A low unit quote can still require more cash if the minimum order is too large. Use the supplier quote comparison to compare the same cost boundary.

Model sales after returns, not just launch revenue

Illustrative first-month trading model
LineCalculationResult
Gross shipped merchandise30 overshirts × $60 + 20 trousers × $50$2,800
Refunds3 overshirts × $60 + 2 trousers × $50−$280
Net revenue2,800 − 280$2,520
Net cost of sold stock27 × $24 + 18 × $20$1,008
Gross profit2,520 − 1,008$1,512
Selling costsFees 90 + packing 100 + outbound 210 + returns 40 + ads 300$740
Contribution before launch setup, tax and owner pay1,512 − 740$772

All five returned garments are assumed checked and resalable. Closing stock is 53 overshirts and 42 trousers: 95 units costing $2,112. Net unit sell-through is 45 ÷ 140 = 32.14%. Damaged or unsellable returns would change stock value and cost; do not use this example without adjusting that assumption.

If all $980 of one-off launch setup were charged against this management period, the $772 contribution would become a $208 shortfall before tax and owner pay. That is an illustration of launch economics, not a claim about formal accounting treatment.

Medium sell-through is 17 ÷ 30 = 56.67%; small and large are each 20%. Reordering the original size ratio would ignore the evidence. Check fit complaints and the small sample size before treating one month as a permanent demand pattern.

Assuming the full $2,520 of net customer receipts is collected in the month and the $740 selling costs are paid separately, closing cash is $6,000 − $4,100 + $2,520 − $740 = $3,680. Do not subtract the $1,008 cost of sold stock again: the opening inventory purchase is already in the $4,100 cash outflow. If your payment processor pays net of fees, reconcile the payout before deducting those fees a second time.

Fill the plan and set 30, 60 and 90-day decisions

Copy these fields: target buyer and evidence; two-style offer; size range and fit notes; supplier quote and minimum order; samples and approvals; opening stock by variant; prices and return assumptions; fulfillment capacity; cash dates; marketing test; owner and review date. Attach the source for each estimate.

By day 30 compare paid orders, retained units, fit feedback and contribution after selling costs. By day 60 compare size-level stock and supplier lead time before authorizing replenishment. By day 90 decide whether to repeat, revise or discontinue each style. Keep the original plan alongside actuals rather than replacing the forecast.

Positioning, acquisition and production checks

Fictional launch decisions to validate
DecisionInitial planEvidence before scaling
Customer and occasionMix-and-match workwear for a narrow local buyer groupFive buyer conversations and fit feedback; record objections
Competitive alternativesExisting wardrobe, secondhand and comparable local labelsCompare fit, total delivered price and availability; do not claim uniqueness without evidence
AcquisitionA fitting event and a small product-content test within the $300 ad allowanceTrack paid retained orders, not only likes or signups
Production gateApprove a size sample before the 140-unit commitmentDocument measurements, defects, delivery and rework responsibility
Operations ownerOwner handles fit questions, packing and returnsTime a pilot order and inspect returned condition

Keep a release calendar with sample approval, production confirmation, photography, stock receipt, launch and first review. Do not announce a delivery date before the supplier schedule and packing capacity support it. If fit feedback requires a pattern change, revise the quote and cash dates before committing the remaining production payment. A new style is a separate inventory decision, not an automatic reward for a busy launch.
