A boutique inventory spreadsheet works best when each size-and-color combination has its own row. Track the units you physically have, the units already promised to customers, and the units still available to sell. A single total for “black T-shirts” cannot tell you whether the medium size is sold out.

This guide walks through a small clothing-store example and shows how to keep a usable stock record without building a complicated workbook. All quantities below are fictional examples, not NexalyPlanner customer results.

Give every sellable variant a stable SKU

A SKU is the identifier your shop uses for an item you stock. Choose a pattern you can repeat, such as product–color–size:

SKUProductColorSize
TEE-BLK-SEveryday teeBlackSmall
TEE-BLK-MEveryday teeBlackMedium
DRS-SAG-MWeekend dressSageMedium
SCF-OAT-OSLightweight scarfOatOne size

Keep the SKU unchanged when you update a product title. Otherwise, an older sale may become difficult to match to the current item. If a supplier uses another code, store that code in a separate note instead of replacing your own identifier.

Your SKU does not need to contain every product detail. A readable, unique identifier is more useful than a long code that nobody can enter consistently.

Separate physical stock from available stock

Two quantities answer different questions:

On hand is the number of sellable units physically in your stock after recorded movements. Available is the number left after subtracting units reserved for confirmed, unfulfilled orders.

For the same reporting period, use:

On hand = opening stock + receipts − shipped units + sellable returns − write-offs

Available = on hand − reserved units

Suppose your small black tee starts with 12 units. You receive eight, ship nine, accept one sellable return and write off two damaged units. You now have 10 sellable units on hand. If three are reserved for confirmed orders, seven remain available.

Do not subtract those three reserved units as shipments until they actually leave stock. On fulfillment, reduce both physical stock and the outstanding reservation. This prevents a single sale from being deducted twice.

Set a clear period for your clothing inventory spreadsheet

The starter sheet is a period summary. Its movement columns hold totals for the period you are tracking; it is not an automatic transaction log. Write the period in your working copy and keep the same start and end dates for every row.

When a new period begins, archive the previous file and carry its closing on-hand balance into the next opening balance. Bring forward only reservations that are still outstanding. Reset receipts, shipments, returns and write-offs for the new period.

If you need to trace every individual movement, keep a dated transaction log alongside the summary. Record the SKU, units, reason and related order number. That history explains a change that a closing balance alone cannot explain.

Build a short stock-check routine

During the selling day, record receipts and shipments when they happen. Before closing, review orders that reserve inventory but have not shipped. Cancelled reservations should no longer block sellable stock.

For a physical check, count one shelf or a small group of variants. Compare your count with the on-hand column, not the available column. Reserved items may still be sitting on that shelf.

If the count differs, investigate recent deliveries, unrecorded shipments, damages and returns. Record an explained adjustment instead of changing a number simply to make the sheet match. Keep damaged returns out of sellable inventory until their condition has been assessed.

Know when a spreadsheet is becoming difficult to maintain

A spreadsheet can be sufficient when a clear owner updates it and the workflow is manageable. Friction grows when several people edit copies, orders arrive through multiple channels, or stock changes have to be re-entered elsewhere.

Frequently asked questions

Can I track boutique inventory in Excel?

Yes. Start with one row per variant, consistent SKUs and clearly defined stock movements. The free workbook includes formulas for on-hand and available units. Keep a backup before changing its structure.

Should I count an unpaid order as sold inventory?

Define a reservation policy first. An unpaid request may be an inquiry, while a confirmed order may reserve stock. Keep reservation, payment and fulfillment states distinct so your numbers reflect your actual process.

How do I track a return?

Record the returned quantity and assess its condition. Add only sellable units back to sellable stock. Keep the refund record separate from the stock movement; a refund and a physical return may happen on different days.

Can this template automatically sync my online store?

No. This is an editable, manually maintained spreadsheet. Automatic store synchronization requires a separately verified integration.

Start by entering the variants you currently sell, count the units you have, and reconcile one completed day before adding more complexity.
