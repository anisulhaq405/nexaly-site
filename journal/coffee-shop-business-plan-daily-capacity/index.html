Coffee Shop Business Plan: Daily Capacity and Cost Example

By NexalyPlanner · Published October 13, 2026

Fictional worked examples

A coffee shop business plan needs a daily operating assumption you can test. Connect expected orders to opening hours and staffing, then compare an ordinary day with a slower day using the same cost definitions. This guide focuses on the operating example named in the title; use it as one supporting part of your wider business plan.

Check the customer and offer before the spreadsheet

Name the customer, buying occasion and intended product mix. A commuter buying one drink creates different preparation and queue demands from a group ordering food and drinks. Observe suitable locations and ask real prospective customers about recent purchases.

Keep notes about pricing, opening hours and alternatives. Those observations help frame assumptions; they do not guarantee demand. The operating worksheet starts after you have chosen an offer and want to inspect its daily arithmetic.

Make the volume assumption physically possible

The fictional shop opens for eight hours and enters a feasible maximum of 20 orders per hour, giving a 160-order daily ceiling. Expected volume is 120 orders. The average of 15 orders per opening hour does not prove the peak queue will work: a busy hour can exceed the average. Check preparation steps, equipment and staffing with a real trial before treating capacity as established. The calculator uses your entered ceiling and flags a base order count above it.

Calculate the ordinary-day example

At 120 orders and $8 average order value, modeled sales are $960. Ingredients and packaging at $2.50 per order cost $300. Two six-hour shifts at $20 employer cost per hour total $240.

Other daily operating costs are $180. The remainder is $960 minus $300 minus $240 minus $180, or $240. These are fictional planning amounts. Include appropriate labor-related costs in the employer-cost input; do not treat the sample rate as a wage benchmark.

Keep staffing visible in the slower day

With 80 orders, sales become $640 and ingredients/packaging $200. The entered staffing remains $240 and other costs remain $180, leaving only $20. The $220 reduction in remainder comes from 40 fewer orders at $5.50 contribution before staffing and other costs. This scenario does not automatically reduce shifts. If you model different staffing, save a separate report and check whether the reduced coverage can still serve the planned work.

Extend the daily worksheet into a business plan

Use the daily model as one operating appendix. A complete plan also needs customer research, premises and equipment assumptions, supplier arrangements, service workflow, marketing and a dated cash plan. Purchase costs, debt principal, tax and owner withdrawals are not in this daily remainder unless clearly entered in the appropriate scope, and a daily remainder is not bank cash. Use the monthly budget guide for totals and a cash forecast for payment dates. Confirm local operating requirements through the appropriate authorities rather than copying a generic checklist.

Questions about the coffee shop worksheet

Is an order the same as a cup? Not necessarily; use a consistent average order value and per-order ingredient cost. Can I include food?

Yes, through coherent blended assumptions, but test the mix separately. Does the worksheet calculate cafe net profit? No; it calculates a bounded daily operating remainder from the costs you enter.
