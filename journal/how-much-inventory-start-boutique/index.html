How much inventory do you need to start a boutique?

By NexalyPlanner · Published September 29, 2026 · Updated November 13, 2026

Back to the NexalyPlanner Journal

How much stock should a boutique start with?

Start with an orderable assortment that fits your cash ceiling—not a universal number of pieces. In this small online-boutique example, 6 styles × 2 colors × 2 sizes × 5 units = 120 pieces. The opening buy costs $2,640 against a $3,500 merchandise ceiling. Different size ranges, costs, store formats or supplier packs change that answer.

Use three checks together: can you fund the order, does the variant mix serve your intended customers, and can you replenish before the items you want to keep selling run out?

1. Set an inventory cash ceiling before counting pieces

Separate the money for garments from the money needed to open and keep operating. The U.S. Small Business Administration’s startup-cost guidance distinguishes one-time and monthly expenses and includes inventory among the costs to plan. Your lease deposit, payroll, website, insurance and other obligations still need funding even when the opening stock looks affordable.

Suppose an owner has $5,000 specifically available for launch buying and setup. Rent, permits, taxes and operating runway have already been funded elsewhere. This is not a claim that $5,000 opens a physical boutique.

Launch cash pot

$5,000

The limited pool used in this example.

Other launch costs

− $900

Assumed photography, packaging and first campaign costs.

Protected reserve

− $600

Set aside for later needs; not available to enlarge this opening order.

Merchandise ceiling

$3,500

$5,000 − $900 − $600.

Do not also put a charge in this $900 allowance if it is already included in merchandise landed cost. Once the $2,640 opening buy is planned, $860 of merchandise headroom remains. That is separate from the protected $600 reserve. The complete allocation is $900 + $2,640 + $860 + $600 = $5,000.

Record supplier deposit, balance and freight due dates. A 30% deposit on a $2,640 order is $792; the remaining $1,848 still needs funding. A small deposit does not make the full commitment smaller. Use the small business budget example for the wider cash plan.

Online boutique, pop-up or permanent store?

  • Online launch: check storage, photography capacity, fulfillment and the customer’s ability to find a complete size option.
  • Pop-up: account for event duration, display capacity, transport and how unsold pieces will be sold afterward.
  • Permanent store: plan fixtures and presentation alongside rent and operating cash. Floor area alone does not prove how much inventory customers will buy.

No reserve percentage or opening quantity in this guide is a universal benchmark. Use your real invoices, terms and operating commitments.

2. Calculate styles, variants and total opening units

A style is a design. A variant is a sellable combination such as one style in black, size M. A unit is one physical piece. Ten designs do not mean ten SKUs when each design comes in several colors and sizes.

Variants = styles × colors × sizes
Units = sum of the quantities for every variant
Opening landed cost = sum of (variant units × landed unit cost)

The fictional launch uses 6 styles, 2 colors and only 2 sizes per color: 24 variants. At five units each, that is 120 units. The two-size range is an arithmetic example, not a recommendation to leave out sizes your audience needs.

The weighted average is $2,640 ÷ 120 = $22 per unit. The $3,500 ceiling less $2,640 leaves $860; the protected $600 reserve stays separate.

A budget divided by unit cost is a ceiling, not a buying target

If every item cost exactly $22 landed, $3,500 ÷ $22 would allow at most 159 whole units, costing $3,498. A 160th unit would take the order to $3,520. But real packs, different category costs, missing sizes and uncertain demand may justify buying substantially less. The 120-piece mix above is one such plan.

Likewise, adding two more sizes everywhere while keeping five units of each variant doubles this example to 48 variants, 240 units and $5,280, assuming the same category prices. If you need broader size coverage, reduce depth, colors or styles and price the revised rows. Do not quietly double the order.

3. Decide size and color depth from evidence

Separate each size/color SKU before placing the order. Start with your own past sales, measured fit feedback, customer requests or a clearly documented test assumption. A social-media poll is weaker evidence than a completed sale; a preorder also carries a delivery commitment. There is no reliable universal size ratio for every boutique.

For a separate size-pack example, suppose one supplier sells six-unit mixed packs containing S: 1, M: 2, L: 2 and XL: 1. One style in two colors, one pack per color, gives 8 variants and 12 units—not six units of every size. At an assumed $22 landed per piece, the two packs cost $264. This supplier ratio is illustrative, not a recommended customer size curve.

  • Use a stable reference such as TOP-01-BLK-M for each sellable variant.
  • Record the actual measurements and fit notes, rather than assuming two suppliers use identical size labels.
  • Keep total quantity, sellable quantity and customer reservations distinct.
  • Count requests for unavailable sizes separately from sales; avoid extrapolating the size mix only from what was in stock.

The free Boutique Size & Color Stock Matrix helps compare counted variants with targets you enter. The boutique inventory spreadsheet guide covers ongoing stock movements. Neither replaces the supplier’s confirmation of an orderable size pack.

4. Check landed costs, supplier packs and payment timing

A lower wholesale price can produce a higher delivered cost. For 60 equivalent pieces, Quote A at $17 each plus $180 freight costs $1,200, or $20 per unit. Quote B at $18 each with freight included costs $1,080, or $18 per unit. The difference is $120, assuming equivalent usable quality, specifications and delivery dates.

For planning, landed cost includes the purchase price plus the relevant charges needed to get usable stock ready for sale. Confirm which freight, insurance, handling, nonrecoverable charges or other amounts belong in your scope. Do not count the same freight invoice twice or treat every tax payment as an inventory cost. The Shipment Cost Allocation Tool can distribute entered shared charges across SKUs; it does not determine customs value or tax treatment.

The six-unit order is (72 × $18) + (48 × $25) + (24 × $28) = $3,168. It fits the cash ceiling, but that alone does not prove you should buy it. The eight-unit order does not fit. Spending the separate $600 reserve would still leave that $4,224 order $124 beyond the combined $4,100, before any other changed costs.

Ask whether the minimum applies per size/color SKU, mixed pack, style, supplier or total dollar order. Shopify’s MOQ guidance explains why supplier rules can include both minimums and order multiples. Negotiate a smaller assortment, mixed pack or another supplier when the actual order does not fit.

Check whether markdowns leave room to sell the stock

At $22 landed cost and a $44 selling price, full-price gross margin is ($44 − $22) ÷ $44 = 50%. A 20% price reduction gives a $35.20 sale price and 37.5% gross margin. If that sale also incurs $5 of selling and fulfillment costs, contribution before fixed overhead is $8.20. These are fictional inputs; inventory affordability does not establish net profit.

5. Review sell-through and stock before reordering

Choose an initial review date that fits your sales activity and replenishment time. Fourteen days is this example’s first checkpoint, not an industry rule or enough evidence for every seasonal category.

Suppose 42 of the 120 opening units shipped, four of those units came back and passed inspection, and three other units became damaged. There have been no new receipts:

Net units sold after returns
42 − 4 = 38
Sellable on hand
120 − 42 + 4 − 3 = 79 units
Available after 5 customer reservations
79 − 5 = 74 units
Opening-cohort net sell-through
38 ÷ 120 × 100 = 31.67%

Gross shipped sell-through would be 42 ÷ 120 = 35%. State which definition you use; do not compare a net figure with a gross target. The 31.67% figure here uses the original opening cohort and no new receipts. New stock, transfers, cancellations and non-sellable returns need separate movements. Use the return-to-stock log to keep inspection decisions visible.

Review each variant. A slow category may need better presentation, a different offer or no reorder. A stockout can hide demand, while an opening discount can temporarily inflate it. Check what sold, what was returned and what customers wanted but could not buy.

Separate the reorder trigger from the order quantity

For a continuously reviewed replenishable SKU selling an assumed two net units per week, with three weeks’ lead time and two chosen safety units, the illustrative trigger is (2 × 3) + 2 = 8 units. Inventory position is sellable on hand plus eligible confirmed incoming units, less unfilled customer commitments. If reservations represent those same commitments, subtract them once.

For a separate weekly-review policy, an order-up-to planning target is 2 × (3 + 1) + 2 = 10 units. At each weekly review, assess the gap to this target. With inventory position of 7, the unrounded gap is 3 units. If orders must be in packs of six, the candidate order becomes 6 units, costing $132 at an assumed $22 landed. Recheck the extra depth, cash due date and season before approving it. This is a manual scenario, not an optimized demand forecast.

The reorder point guide covers the trigger in more detail, and the Stock Coverage Planner helps review the assumptions you enter. A one-off trend style may intentionally sell out rather than be replenished.

6. Use a written buy/no-buy checklist

Approve the opening order only when every item below has an answer:

  • The actual SKU/size/color quantities match supplier packs and minimums.
  • Total landed commitments fit the merchandise ceiling without taking protected operating cash.
  • Deposit, balance, freight and other due dates have cash allocated.
  • Storage, photography, product descriptions and fulfillment capacity cover the assortment.
  • Returns, damaged units and reservations can be recorded separately.
  • A first review date and a repeat/no-repeat decision are assigned.

Frequently asked questions about boutique opening stock

How many pieces should a small boutique start with?

Work from your cash ceiling, variant plan, supplier packs and demand assumptions. The fictional online launch in this guide starts with 120 pieces across 24 variants at $2,640 landed cost. It is an example, not a minimum for every boutique.

Can I start a boutique with $5,000?

It depends on which costs that amount must cover. This example allocates $900 to other launch costs, $600 to a protected reserve and $3,500 to merchandise, but assumes rent, taxes and operating runway are funded separately. It is not a complete physical-store startup budget.

How many clothing styles should I buy initially?

Choose enough styles to test a clear assortment without leaving the sizes you need unfunded. Count colors and sizes as separate variants. Six styles in two colors and two sizes already create 24 variants; five pieces of each means 120 units.

Should every size and color get the same quantity?

Only if that is your documented test assumption and the supplier allows it. Use your own fit feedback, sales and customer requests to revise depth. A mixed six-unit size pack is not the same as six pieces of every size.

How do I calculate an opening inventory budget?

Subtract other launch commitments, protected operating cash and your chosen reserve from the relevant cash pool. Then sum each SKU quantity multiplied by its landed unit cost and confirm the order fits. Check deposits and future balance payments as well as cash due immediately.

What if the supplier minimum is larger than my plan?

Recalculate the actual packs and total landed commitment. Consider fewer styles or colors, a different mixed pack or another supplier. Do not use protected operating cash simply to meet a minimum.

When should I reorder boutique inventory?

Review demand by SKU, sellable stock, commitments, eligible incoming stock, lead time, cash and seasonality. A reorder trigger is not the order quantity. In the fictional example, an eight-unit trigger and a ten-unit order-up-to target produce a three-unit gap at inventory position seven, then a six-unit candidate if the supplier requires packs of six.

Are the inventory amounts in this article industry averages?

No. The prices, quantities, size ratios, reserves and sales figures are hypothetical examples. Replace them with supplier quotes, customer evidence and the actual obligations of your own business.

Sources and calculation method

The calculations, assortment and review examples are original hypothetical scenarios. They are not survey findings, a sales forecast or an estimate of your local supplier prices.
