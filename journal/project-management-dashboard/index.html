Create a project management dashboard with weighted progress, forecast cost, role capacity and delivery dates. Includes a reconciled project example.

Example basis: All businesses, amounts and outcomes below are fictional planning examples in USD, prepared November 4, 2026. They are not industry benchmarks or customer results.

Choose the decisions before the charts

A project management dashboard should tell a reviewer what has been accepted, whether the delivery date is still credible, what completion is likely to cost, and which decision is blocking work. A task count alone is too weak: finishing two small tasks does not mean half a project is delivered.

For a single project, use accepted deliverables, baseline versus forecast dates, actual plus remaining cost, capacity by role, and an exception list. A portfolio dashboard adds a separate comparison across projects. Keep the scope and audience explicit so an executive summary does not expose unnecessary client or staff details.

Calculate progress from accepted work

Weighted progress is the sum of weight × accepted completion: 10 + 15 + 22 + 0 = 47%. The weights describe this fictional scope, not a universal standard. Define how a partial build earns acceptance; a developer’s estimate of effort spent is not equivalent to a client accepting functionality.

Two of four deliverables are complete, so the raw count is 50%. That is a different measure. If the baseline expected 60 weighted points by this cutoff, actual progress trails by 13 percentage points. Avoid reporting “13% late”: relative percentages, schedule days and percentage points are different quantities.

Spending 56% of the budget does not establish 56% completion; accepted progress is 47%. Do not calculate remaining cost as budget minus spend when the delivery estimate says otherwise. A $5,900 remaining estimate requires a forecast of $11,500 even when the approved budget is still $10,000.

Show scope changes with their approval date and value. Preserve the original baseline so an approved change is visible rather than rewriting history to make every variance disappear. The date comparison above uses calendar days, not working days or a holiday calendar.

Capacity must match the role

The net six-hour shortfall understates the delivery problem if the designer cannot do the developer’s work. The developer is eight hours short. Resolve that role constraint through a scoped reduction, a qualified reassignment or a revised date; do not treat two spare design hours as interchangeable development capacity.

Availability should already exclude approved leave, recurring support and other commitments. Use estimates for the same time window as the capacity total. A monthly availability number cannot validate a deadline next Friday. For multi-client work, compare the agency profitability and capacity planner against your actual process.

Copyable dashboard fields and review agenda

For each deliverable record: ID, owner, baseline date, forecast date, weight, acceptance criterion, evidence link, accepted completion, remaining hours, cost rate and last-updated date. Record a blocker’s decision owner and due date separately. Keep detailed notes in the project record and show only exceptions on the dashboard.

At review, first check stale data and missing acceptance evidence. Then discuss the critical blocker, the role shortfall and the forecast budget variance. End with decisions and dates. In this example an approval due November 6 needs an owner; marking the card amber without assigning a decision does not resolve the delay.

Can I use a spreadsheet? Yes, if formulas, permissions and version history fit the team. This guide describes a manual review structure, not automatic Asana or other platform integration. Should every project use these weights? No. Agree them before measuring progress and retain a dated record when the scope changes.
