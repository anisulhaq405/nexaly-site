Write a landscape maintenance business plan with crew-hours, travel, weekly visit capacity, costs and collection timing. Includes a weather-delay scenario.

Example basis: Businesses, amounts, fees and outcomes in the worked examples are fictional planning assumptions in USD, prepared November 5, 2026. They are not market averages, observed customer results or income promises.

Choose a service and a route boundary

A landscape business plan should specify the services, customer area, crew, equipment, visit capacity, costs and payment timing. This example is recurring landscape maintenance, not landscape architecture, construction or chemical application. Those services can require different skills, obligations and equipment.

The fictional company serves a small local route with two paid crew members. Its initial offer is a clearly bounded maintenance visit. Before advertising, the owner must verify the local service requirements, insurance and any applicable professional permissions with the responsible official sources. This example does not establish those requirements or recommend a wage rate.

A crew-clock hour means both people working for one elapsed hour. Two people paid for 40 hours each provide 80 labor hours, not 80 elapsed hours on the route. Adding both measures would double-count capacity.

At an estimated 1.1 crew-clock hours per visit, 22 onsite hours support 20 visits. Validate the estimate with actual property scope, access, travel and cleanup. Keep the four-hour weather reserve unbooked until it is needed; selling those hours in advance removes the buffer. Equipment availability and skills can impose a tighter constraint than total hours.

The $16 fully loaded hourly cost and $85 visit price are fictional assumptions, not employment guidance or local market rates. Include actual wages, employer costs and applicable obligations in the real estimate. The $180 is a stated period amount; it is not the purchase price of a truck or a complete equipment-replacement reserve.

The listed cost per completed visit is $1,660 ÷ 20 = $83 before owner pay, tax and omitted costs. A price of $85 leaves only $2 per visit under these assumptions. Raising the assumed price to $100 would model a $340 weekly remainder at the same 20 visits, but customer acceptance and demand might change. Test price and scope with evidence instead of treating the arithmetic as a sales forecast.

Model weather and collection timing

Opening cash is $3,000. If only 80% of the $1,700 revenue is collected in the week, receipts are $1,360 and $340 remains receivable. Paying all $1,660 of listed costs leaves $2,700. The operating remainder is positive $40 while cash falls $300 because part of the work is uncollected.

The delayed visits are moved, not silently cancelled. Their $255 revenue belongs to the future completion period under this model, while wages and other stated period costs remain. Saved consumables are only $12. The next week must have room for the deferred work; do not promise the same hours to new customers.

Keep a dated cash forecast for deposits, payroll, invoices and equipment purchases. Receivables are not bank cash. If a customer disputes scope, the plan also needs a documented acceptance and follow-up process.

By day 30, replace the 1.1-hour estimate with timed visits and compare travel by route. By day 60, check whether recurring visits leave capacity for delays, repairs and quoting. By day 90, decide whether a denser route, different scope, changed price or reduced booking count improves the real result. Do not add a second crew simply because inquiries increased.
