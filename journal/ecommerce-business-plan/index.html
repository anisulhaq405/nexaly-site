Ecommerce Business Plan: Orders, Returns & Cash Example

Build an ecommerce business plan around conversion, fulfillment, returns, contribution and payment timing. Follow a reconciled 120-order example.

Example basis: All businesses, amounts and outcomes below are fictional planning examples in USD, prepared November 4, 2026. They are not industry benchmarks or customer results.

Start with the offer and operating limits

An ecommerce business plan should explain what you sell, who buys it, why the offer is credible, and how orders turn into delivered goods and collected cash. A traffic target is only one input. Cancellation, returns, fulfillment cost and payment timing determine whether the plan can fund its next cycle.

This fictional store sells one $50 product. It begins with a supplier quote, a 300-unit opening purchase and a monthly traffic hypothesis. Before launch, its owner must verify product demand, delivery promises, stock availability, customer support workload and the published return process. These are planning checks, not evidence that the product will sell.

Keep each stage of the order funnel separate

Full returns after shipment6All six units resalable
Retained orders1021.02% of sessions

The model assumes one item per order. If customers buy multiple units, replace the unit-cost model with item-level records. The paid conversion rate is 120 ÷ 10,000 = 1.20%; retained-order conversion is 102 ÷ 10,000 = 1.02%. They are not interchangeable. Also record whether returns belong to an original-order cohort or the processing period.

Do not add orders claimed by multiple advertising platforms and call the sum unique sales. Use an agreed order-level attribution rule, deduplicate records and preserve a separate view of platform-reported claims.

The remainder excludes tax, owner pay and finance costs. Payment fees here are a fictional $180 total, not a provider rate. Cancellation handling, reshipping, chargebacks and damaged returns are zero in the example; add them when present. A contribution number is useful only when its cost boundary is clear.

Check the fulfillment estimate against hours and provider quotes. If 108 shipments take eight minutes each, direct packing alone needs 864 minutes, or 14.4 hours, before support and returns. A revenue forecast does not create that capacity.

Reconcile inventory and cash

The opening purchase costs 300 × $20 = $6,000. Closing inventory is 300 − 108 + 6 = 198 resalable units, costing $3,960. Net cost of sold stock is $2,040; it is an expense measure, while the $6,000 purchase is the cash commitment.

Here receipts are shown before separately paid fees. If actual processor payouts already deduct fees, refunds or reserves, reconcile them to gross movements rather than subtracting twice. The pending $1,020 is not in closing cash. A delayed payout changes cash timing even when retained-order revenue remains $5,100.

Use a dated 13-week cash forecast to test supplier payment dates and delayed collections. Include existing liabilities and restricted amounts before deciding cash is available for another stock order.

Copyable business-plan outline and decision rules

Write: executive summary; customer problem and evidence; product, price and supplier terms; sales channels and traffic assumptions; conversion and return definitions; stock and fulfillment capacity; cost model; receipt and payment dates; downside case; responsible owner and review dates. Use the cover-page builder only as the document front sheet, not as a substitute for these sections.

In the first review, compare actual paid orders, cancellations, shipped units and returned condition. In the second, inspect contribution by product and channel using deduplicated orders. Before replenishment, confirm the supplier commitment fits the cash forecast if the pending $1,020 arrives late. Keep the original forecast to learn which assumption failed.

Can one plan cover every store? No. Subscription, dropshipping, stocked goods and made-to-order models have different payment and inventory risks. This example is a stocked single-item model. Does positive contribution mean profitable growth? Not by itself; overhead, repeat buying, taxes and cash commitments still matter.

Market evidence, acquisition and fulfillment controls

Fictional store planning decisions

AlternativesComparable product, delivered price and delivery promiseDated comparison; no invented competitor defects
Acquisition test$900 maximum across explicitly named channelsDeduplicated orders and actual spend, not platform totals added together
FulfillmentShip from the 300-unit opening purchaseStock receipt checks, dispatch times and exception log
Customer supportOwner answers pre-sale and return questionsSupport time and recurring confusion by product

Write the promised dispatch window only after checking receiving, packing and carrier handoff. If a supplier delay means orders cannot be fulfilled as promised, revise the public promise and customer handling process rather than treating an order as complete when a label is created. Test one variable at a time where practical: changing price, channel, audience and shipping promise simultaneously makes it hard to explain a result. The plan should name who can stop spending when the cash or fulfillment assumption fails.
