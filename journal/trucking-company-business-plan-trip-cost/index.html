Trucking Company Business Plan: Total-Mile Cost Example

By NexalyPlanner · Published October 13, 2026

Fictional worked examples

A trucking company business plan needs a consistent mileage denominator. Compare revenue and costs across the same trip, including empty miles, and inspect the payment delay separately from the modeled operating remainder. This guide focuses on the operating example named in the title; use it as one supporting part of your wider business plan.

Define one trip before estimating annual demand

Record the route, loaded miles, repositioning or return miles and the agreed revenue scope. Distinguish a known job from a hoped-for load. Use actual quotes, vehicle records and your operating assumptions for costs. The free worksheet is a trip model, not dispatch software, a regulatory checklist or a full trucking feasibility study. A wider business plan also needs customer acquisition, equipment capacity, maintenance, insurance and an appropriate operating review.

Calculate fuel over all miles

The fictional trip covers 400 loaded miles and 100 empty miles, totaling 500. At 6.25 miles per gallon, expected fuel use is 80 gallons. At the entered $4 per gallon, modeled fuel cost is $320.

These are illustrative assumptions, not current fuel prices or fleet benchmarks. Check the vehicle's relevant fuel history and the actual route before using them. Traffic, terrain, load and idling can change consumption; the worksheet does not simulate those factors.

Add allowances and other trip costs once

Maintenance allowance at $0.20 per total mile adds $100. Entered nonfuel costs are $300 driver cost, $50 tolls and $100 fixed operating allocation, totaling $450. Modeled trip cost is $320 plus $100 plus $450, or $870.

Against $1,500 trip revenue, the operating remainder is $630. Do not also enter the calculated fuel or maintenance allowance as a nonfuel row, which would double-count them. Include every relevant cost in a coherent scope before interpreting the remainder.

Compare per-mile figures with the same denominator

Cost per total mile is $870 divided by 500, or $1.74. Revenue per total mile is $3.00. Revenue per loaded mile is $3.75 because only 400 miles are paid in this example.

Comparing $3.75 loaded-mile revenue against a cost figure built from all 500 miles can obscure the effect of empty travel. State the denominator every time. If loaded miles are zero, revenue per loaded mile is undefined rather than zero.

Inspect the collection gap separately

The sample nonfuel payments are dated October 16 and expected collection is October 30. The report lists those entered payments before collection. Fuel dates and maintenance cash timing are not modeled: a maintenance reserve or fixed allocation may not be cash spent on this trip. Put actual fuel payments, payroll, bills and receipts into a dated cash planner before concluding that the job is funded. Use the existing maintenance-due planner to check entered service intervals, not as a trucking dispatch or safety certification tool.

Questions about trucking trip cost

Can empty miles change a quoted margin? Yes; include them in fuel, maintenance and total-mile calculations. Does the report calculate net profit?

No; it shows the bounded remainder from entered assumptions. Does a collection date confirm payment? No; keep evidence and follow-up in your invoice collection schedule.

Compare fuel price and empty-mile scenarios

These are separate fictional changes to the 500-mile sample, not current market rates. At $4.50 per gallon instead of $4.00, 80 gallons cost $360: total modeled cost becomes $910 and the $1,500 trip leaves $590. Reset fuel to $4.00, then increase empty miles from 100 to 150. Total distance becomes 550 miles; fuel costs $352, maintenance allowance is $110 and other entered costs stay $450. Total cost is $912, leaving $588. Review whether driver, toll and fixed allocations also need to change before using that second scenario.
