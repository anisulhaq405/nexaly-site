Freelance Business Plan: Capacity, Pricing and a 90-Day Example

NexalyPlanner Journal · Business Planning · Published September 28, 2026

Back to the NexalyPlanner Journal

By NexalyPlanner · Published September 28, 2026 · Updated October 1, 2026

A freelance business plan works best when it starts with billable capacity rather than a revenue wish. This example uses a fictional independent designer who has 80 working hours available each month. The figures are illustrative USD assumptions.

Quick answer: specify the client problem, offer, price, capacity, acquisition channel, payment terms and a cash reserve. Review projects and unpaid invoices every week.

Define the offer and the limits

The designer offers a fixed-scope landing-page design package for $1,250, estimated at 16 delivery hours plus 4 hours of discovery and revisions. Three packages per month would produce $3,750 invoiced revenue and use 60 hours. The remaining 20 hours cover proposals, admin and unexpected revisions. The effective planned rate is $3,750 ÷ 60 client hours = $62.50; it is below a quoted $75 hourly alternative because the package includes non-design work.

Do not promise four packages simply because 80 ÷ 20 = 4. That would leave no time for selling, bookkeeping or delays. The SBA’s business-plan outline provides a larger framework; a solo operator still needs an explicit capacity constraint.

If one client pays a month late, $1,250 of invoices may not be available to pay current bills. Track booked work, invoices sent and cash collected as separate numbers. The IRS recordkeeping guidance explains why the underlying income and expense evidence needs its own system.

Run a 90-day experiment

Month 1: interview five prospective clients, price the package and complete one pilot with a written scope. Month 2: publish two concrete case studies only with client permission and track 12 qualified inquiries. Month 3: compare actual delivery hours with the 20-hour estimate. If an average package takes 26 hours, three projects use 78 hours and the current plan is overloaded. Raise the price, narrow scope or reduce the booking target.

Test the price against hours actually delivered

The $1,250 package is only attractive if scope stays controlled. Suppose one project takes 16 planned design hours, four planned discovery and revision hours, plus six unexpected revision hours. Its 26 hours produce $48.08 per hour before direct costs ($1,250 ÷ 26). At that pace three projects consume 78 hours, leaving just two hours for proposals and admin. A fourth client would create a delivery promise the calendar cannot support. Write the included revision rounds and the change-request process into the offer.

Cash timing needs a separate rule. If clients pay 50% at booking and 50% on delivery, three booked packages may bring in $1,875 initially, not $3,750. The plan should list each milestone, expected payment date and follow-up owner. If one project slips, the second payment slips too. Do not spend against an invoice that has not been collected.

What to review on Friday

Compare qualified inquiries, proposals, wins, hours estimated versus actual, open client commitments and unpaid invoices. If the win rate drops, inspect the client fit and proposal terms before simply increasing outreach volume. If delivery hours rise, reduce bookings or change the package scope. Record client permission before turning work into a case study, and keep confidential project material outside public marketing.

The complete freelancer plan: first client through repeatable delivery

Step 1 — Identify a specific client and a paid problem

The fictional designer serves small service businesses whose landing page is unclear or hard to update. She interviews five prospects about recent launches, lost inquiries, decision makers, budgets and approval delays. She reviews three competing offers and writes one outcome in plain language: a clear landing-page design ready for implementation, with an agreed number of pages and revision rounds. A client’s interest in a free call is not demand for the $1,250 package; the first paid pilot tests that assumption.

Step 2 — Define the offer, scope and acceptance

The package includes a 45-minute discovery session, one page structure, a visual design, one handoff file and two consolidated revision rounds. The scope lists the client’s required copy, brand assets and approval contact, plus what is excluded: development, ongoing advertising and extra pages. A written proposal states price, milestones, payment timing, delivery dates, change-request pricing and what counts as final acceptance. Those terms should be checked against the actual contract; the planner merely tracks them.

Step 3 — Price against capacity, not an optimistic calendar

At $1,250 for 20 planned client hours, the package yields $62.50 per planned client hour before expenses. Three packages need 60 hours, leaving 20 of 80 working hours for prospecting, admin and buffer. Allocate those 20 explicitly: eight for inquiries/proposals, four for finance/admin, four for learning and four for delay. If each project instead needs 26 hours, three need 78 and only two hours remain. At that point, pause new bookings, narrow scope or raise price rather than silently sacrificing delivery.

Each inquiry has a source, client need, next action and follow-up date. Each accepted project gets a kickoff checklist, client-asset deadline, design block, review date and invoice milestone. She does not count an unsigned proposal as booked revenue or an invoice as collected cash.

Step 5 — Plan cash and the first 90 days

Three projects invoice $3,750. The illustrative $600 of software and contractor costs leaves $3,150 before other overhead, taxes and owner compensation. If each client pays half at booking and half at acceptance, only $1,875 may arrive at booking; collectability and delivery dates determine the other half. For a sample allocation after all $3,750 is actually collected, she earmarks $600 for the stated direct costs, $900 for a tax/contingency reserve chosen for this example and $1,000 for owner withdrawal, leaving $1,250. The $900 is not a recommended tax percentage.

Days 1–30: interview five prospects, document the package and complete one paid pilot. Days 31–60: refine the scope from actual hours, request permitted referrals and aim for 12 qualified inquiries. Days 61–90: compare inquiry-to-proposal, proposal-to-win, estimated-to-actual hours, acceptance delays and cash collected. If one $1,250 final payment is late, revise the cash calendar before committing the next contractor expense. Retain a dated version of the plan and change one assumption at a time.

Run the client-delivery checklist for every project

Before kickoff, confirm the decision maker, source files, written scope, invoice contact and review dates. At kickoff, map the page sections and define the one conversion action. During design, log hours against the 20-hour estimate in discovery, production and revisions separately. Send one consolidated review request and record the client’s approval or change request. At acceptance, deliver the agreed handoff files, issue the final invoice and ask for a short retrospective.

A simple status board can use Inquiry → Qualified → Proposal sent → Accepted → In production → Client review → Accepted/paid. Every item needs a next action and date. A client waiting for copy stays in a blocked state rather than consuming a fictional design block. If two clients both delay approval by a week, the owner checks whether the following month’s 60 client hours are still possible before taking another deposit.

The monthly decision sheet records projected versus actual hours per package, cash received versus invoiced, proposal wins and reasons for lost deals. For example, if the three packages take 18, 22 and 26 hours, the average is 22; three at that pace need 66 hours, leaving 14 of the 80-hour month. The next plan should use 22 hours or tighten scope rather than keeping a convenient 20-hour estimate.

Separate client hours from a full working calendar

In the existing 80-hour monthly example, the three packages taking 18, 22 and 26 hours consume 66 hours. That leaves 14 for selling, administration and buffer, compared with the original 20-hour allowance. A fourth package at the observed 22-hour average would require 88 client hours before any administrative work. Decline or defer the extra commitment unless scope or available time genuinely changes. Record time by discovery, production and revision so you can identify the cause instead of raising every estimate equally. The agency capacity example extends this check to several people; keep each person's available hours separate before calculating a team total.
