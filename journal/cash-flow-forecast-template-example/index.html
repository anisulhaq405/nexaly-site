Cash Flow Forecast Template: A 13-Week Worked Example

NexalyPlanner Journal · Financial Planning · Published September 28, 2026

Back to the NexalyPlanner Journal

By NexalyPlanner · Published September 28, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026 · Updated October 1, 2026

A cash flow forecast lists when money is expected to enter and leave the business. It answers a different question from a profit statement: can the business meet payments on the dates they fall due? This 13-week structure works in a spreadsheet or a dedicated planner. The figures are illustrative USD assumptions.

Quick answer: start with bank cash, add expected customer receipts by payment date, subtract payroll, suppliers, tax set-asides and other payments by due date, then carry closing cash into the next week.

Use one row for each expected cash movement

For a fictional small shop, opening cash is $5,000. In week 1 it expects $2,800 from customers and pays $900 to suppliers, $400 for rent and $200 for other bills. Closing cash is $5,000 + $2,800 − $1,500 = $6,300. This $1,300 net inflow says nothing by itself about profit: the stock may have been bought earlier and the receipts may relate to prior sales.

In a spreadsheet, closing cash for each column is opening + receipts − payments, and the next column’s opening equals the preceding closing. Extend the same five lines through week 13. Label uncertain receipts separately; do not count an unsigned quote as money in the bank.

Stress-test the lowest week

If $1,200 of week 2 receipts moves to week 5, week 2 closing becomes $3,600 rather than $4,800, and week 3 closing becomes $3,000 rather than $4,200. The owner can plan supplier timing or an authorized funding source before the shortfall becomes urgent. Keep a minimum cash threshold that reflects actual obligations; do not treat a generic percentage as a universal rule.

Confirm receipts and payments against bank and bookkeeping records; the planner is a decision aid, not the books.

Update on the same weekday

Replace last week’s forecast with actual cash movements, explain large differences, and add a new thirteenth week. Note the date, source and owner of each assumption. If the gap is a late invoice, assign a follow-up; if it is a recurring cost, revise future weeks rather than hiding it in a one-time adjustment.

Use a spreadsheet layout that survives updates

Put weeks 1–13 across the columns and payment categories down the rows. Keep “expected” and “confirmed” customer receipts separate. A confirmed invoice is still not collected cash; use the expected bank date, then replace it with the actual date when paid. List supplier invoices, payroll, rent, taxes and loan payments individually when timing matters. A single “other” row can conceal the week that causes a shortfall.

Freeze the first row with week-ending dates, and use one formula pattern across all 13 columns: closing balance equals opening balance plus receipts minus payments. Check that week 2 opening matches week 1 closing. A negative projected week calls for a concrete action and date, not a negative sign left in a cell. Keep a copy of each week’s forecast so changes in expected receipts can be traced.

Compare actuals with the previous forecast

Suppose week 1 ended at $5,900 rather than $6,300. The $400 gap could be an unpaid customer receipt, an unexpected $400 bill or two smaller differences. Reconcile each row to bank activity and update the future timing. If an overdue invoice is rescheduled to week 3, avoid adding it as a new sale there; it is the same receipt moving dates. The IRS recordkeeping guidance describes maintaining transaction evidence separately from a planning forecast.

Build the 13-week forecast from a bank balance to a weekly decision

Step 1 — Set the starting cash and source records

On the chosen Monday, reconcile the $5,000 opening amount to the business bank account. List customer invoices and expected collection dates, recurring bills, supplier payment terms, payroll, tax payments and known one-off purchases. Record an owner and source for each line. A sales target, unsigned quote or issued invoice is not cash in the bank. If the business has restricted funds, identify them separately rather than treating all account balances as freely spendable.

Step 2 — Lay out every week and calculate closing cash

Create 13 columns with week-ending dates and rows for opening cash, confirmed receipts, uncertain receipts, supplier payments, payroll, occupancy, tax, debt and other commitments. The compact table below rolls categories into total receipts and payments for readability. Week 1 closes at $6,300. Week 2 opens at exactly $6,300, and the same carry-forward rule applies across the horizon.

Every closing figure equals opening + receipts − payments; the $4,100 at week 13 is a forecast, not an ending bank statement. Weeks 10 and 12 are the lowest baseline closing weeks at $3,700. A reserve threshold should reflect actual upcoming commitments, not an arbitrary universal percentage. The detailed rows behind the totals tell the owner what can be changed.

Step 3 — Add a downside case and an action owner

Move a $1,200 receipt from week 2 to week 5, without duplicating it. Week 2 closes at $3,600, week 3 at $3,000 and week 4 at $3,000; week 5 returns to the baseline $4,700 once that receipt arrives. If the owner sets a $3,500 internal cash threshold for this example, weeks 3 and 4 breach it. The action is specific: confirm the invoice collection date, review a discretionary purchase that can genuinely move, and agree any supplier change rather than assuming a bill can simply be delayed. Record the responsible person and decision date.

Step 4 — Roll the forecast each Friday

Replace the past week’s projected entries with actual bank movements, explain material differences and add a new week 13. Keep the prior forecast snapshot; otherwise the accuracy of collection and payment assumptions disappears. Check opening-to-closing carryover, duplicate receipts, tax dates and payments made on weekends. The CashFlow 13 planner can support the rolling view, while the bank and bookkeeping records remain the financial source of truth.

Audit the formula and collection assumptions

In a spreadsheet, place the opening amount above each week’s receipts and payments. A sample formula is closing = opening + sum(receipts) - sum(payments); the next week’s opening cell should point to the previous closing cell. Protect formulas from accidental typing, label USD and week-ending dates, and keep a separate column or comment for confidence and source. The 13-week table’s first four weeks can be checked by hand: $5,000 + $2,800 − $1,500 = $6,300, then $6,300 + $1,700 − $3,200 = $4,800.

When a $1,200 collection is delayed, move the same invoice line from week 2 to week 5 and retain its identifier. Do not create a fresh $1,200 sale in week 5. Compare forecast with bank actuals every Friday, classify each variance as timing, amount or missing item, and revise future weeks. A late receipt is a timing issue; a disputed invoice may also change the amount. Treat those risks differently.

The weekly decision record should name the projected lowest balance, the required minimum for known obligations, the owner of an action and its due date. If a projected payment cannot be met, seek qualified advice and discuss options with the actual counterparty early. A forecast warns of a date-specific gap; it does not authorize withholding a payment.

Connect the forecast to your operating plan

Compare planned receipts and supplier payments with your business budget and actual costs. If client work depends on available delivery hours, use the agency capacity example to check the workload behind the revenue assumption. Keep those assumptions separate from confirmed collection dates.

Put supplier payments on their actual due dates

A purchase order is a commitment record, not proof that cash has left the bank. Suppose a fictional $800 order requires a $200 deposit in week 1 and a $600 balance in week 3. Put those two payments into their expected weeks. If the supplier agrees to cancel five $8 units, confirm the revised invoice and payment terms before reducing the forecast by $40; the remaining due date may still be unchanged. Keep payment evidence outside the quantity calculation. A purchase order tracker shows accepted units and unresolved replacements, while the forecast shows when the agreed payments affect cash. At Friday review, reconcile the deposit and balance to actual payment records and carry any unresolved amount into the next review.
