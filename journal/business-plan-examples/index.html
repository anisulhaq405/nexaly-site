Choose by the business constraint, not the industry label

Business plan examples are most useful when the operating model resembles your work. A stocked shop commits cash before goods sell; a service business sells limited hours; a rental business earns from reusable equipment and available dates. Copying another business’s revenue target does not solve those differences.

This directory links original NexalyPlanner worked guides, not real customer results or ready-made lender approvals. Their amounts are fictional assumptions and each guide explains its cost boundary. Use an example to learn the structure, then replace every price, quantity, hour and date with evidence from your own plan.

Compare three models before choosing a template

Operating-model comparison
ModelFirst useful calculationCash questionEvidence needed
Stocked productsUnits × price, returns and cost of retained salesWhen is stock paid for versus sold and collected?Supplier quote, stock count and returns condition
Client servicesAccepted scope × hours versus delivery capacityWhen are deposits, milestones and final invoices paid?Written scope, actual hours and payment terms

A business can combine models. A designer selling physical samples has both delivery hours and stock. An equipment business offering staffed setup also has labor capacity. Keep those lines separate instead of forcing all costs into one average margin.

Worked adaptation: why profit and cash can disagree

Suppose a fictional shop buys 100 units at $12 each, paying $1,200. It sells 40 at $25, collecting all $1,000 immediately. Cost of sold stock is $480, leaving $520 gross profit before other costs, while the purchase-versus-receipt cash movement is negative $200. The remaining 60 units cost $720 and are not bank cash.

A service business with the same $1,000 invoice could face a different problem: no stock purchase, but the work may consume more hours than available and the client may pay next month. That is why the example must match the business mechanism, not merely the desired revenue.

Before copying a plan, mark its selling unit, cost treatment, owner-time assumption, collection timing and excluded items. Recalculate the example once to understand it, then create a dated version with your own inputs. Keep the source example separate so fictional amounts do not become accidental business facts.

Move from an example to an evidence-backed plan

Write a short summary of the customer, offer and reason to buy. List alternatives and evidence of demand. Map suppliers or delivery stages, name the owner, estimate costs and available hours, then build a dated cash forecast. Add one downside scenario and a review date. If financing is involved, follow the recipient’s actual document requirements rather than assuming a short worksheet is enough.

Use the editable business-plan template and PDF worksheet for the narrative, the startup evidence checklist for actions, and the cover-page builder for document identification. These are distinct jobs: an example explains, a template captures your assumptions, and a checklist tracks supporting work.

Can I submit an example unchanged? No: its business, figures and evidence are fictional. Does the directory include every possible industry? No. It includes the verified linked models and can expand as useful guides become live. Does a balanced calculation prove the business will succeed? No. Demand, execution, obligations and uncertainty remain. Record those uncertainties instead of hiding them behind a polished document.
