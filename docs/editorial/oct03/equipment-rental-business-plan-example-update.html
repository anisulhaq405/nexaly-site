Check the plan against maintenance-adjusted capacity

A booking forecast needs an asset-level availability assumption. In an additional fictional October scenario, a kit has 31 calendar days, three days blocked for service and two days reserved for turnaround with no overlap between those blocks. That leaves 26 days on which the kit can be dispatched. Twelve rented days represent 12 ÷ 26 = 46.15% of those dispatch-eligible days, compared with 12 ÷ 31 = 38.71% of all calendar days. State which denominator the plan uses and keep completed bookings separate from rented-day counts.

A weekend booking that crosses several days can consume more capacity than a one-day pickup. Two jobs on the same date may be feasible with different units, but not with the same kit before turnaround is complete. Build the forecast from actual asset/date windows instead of multiplying a monthly utilization percentage by every item you own.

Use the rental maintenance log and availability example to document service holds, actual release and booking conflicts. If a $135 repair is paid this month, put the payment in the cash forecast. A missed booking affects forecast receipts; do not also treat its full price as another cash payment. Where a future cancellation remains uncertain, show it as a scenario rather than an incurred expense.

A decision before buying the next unit

List requests you could not fulfill, the exact dates and why they were declined: no capacity, unit under service, unsuitable specification or customer cancellation. Extra equipment solves only some of those reasons. If four requests all concern the same unavailable weekend, model a second unit against that evidence. If the existing kit is idle most other dates, more stock can increase purchase and storage costs without fixing demand. Assign a review date and retain the observed requests alongside the forecast.
